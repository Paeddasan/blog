An Experiment on Attention

Author

Peter Raidl

Published

July 7, 2026

In previous posts, I describe the difference between Training and Movement Practice. The goal of training is to drive very specific adaptations with as much input as necessary but as little as possible. Training is an efficiency game. Movement practices are less goal-directed and more experience-oriented. They are not driven by efficiency. The difference comes down to the intention of each session and the overall process.

This post dives into attention during human movement. First, I discuss a connection between dual-processing theory and top-down motor control. Then I lay out a movement practice to explore shifting attention. It is based on willingly switching between internal and external focus of attention 1. I like the presented practice because of its simplicity. It can help break up patterns and find new ways to engage with movement.

Attention drives Movement - Movement drives Attention

When I think of human movement, my intuition is to think of manipulating the relationship between the body, or its segments, and the environment. But this is only a small fraction of human movement. Human movement is a broad spectrum of changes in shape and position. It ranges from the microscopic folding of proteins, contraction of blood vessels, and the expansion of the lungs to whole-body rearrangements in space.

Movement is purposeful when it all serves the system’s goals - like preserving life - but there are also random elements to it (Rosenblueth et al. 1943). There are qualia to many, but not all of our movements. Some internal states are closed to conscious experience or allegedly only available to the most experienced yogis. Most experientially accessible movements have a bidirectional relation with attention. The purpose and intention of movement direct attention. Solutions emerge through this interplay. Attention is active and causal to action. At the same time, attention can be passive when it is affected by movement and its sensory feedback (Fernandez-Duque and Johnson 2002).

Organization of movement is based on multiple parallel and serial processes. One of these processes is based on purpose- and intention-based attention. But its activity use depends on the situation. As popularized by Daniel Kahneman, Dual-processing theories distinguish between System 1 (“thinking fast”) and System 2 (“thinking slow”), (2012)) 2. According to this theory, there are two modes or systems of cognition. System 1 is the fast, intuitive, automatic mode. Under System 1, action is built on heuristics and learned associations. System 2 is the slow mode of argumentation and logic. System 2 is not error-free or morally “better”, but it helps break up patterns and find new solutions, in case our typical responses don’t work. Dividing cognition into two distinct systems as a strong simplification (Evans and Stanovich 2013), but it’s useful in describing decisions. Samuel et al. (2026) apply this framework to a broad range of psychological “adaptations”3. Here I will specifically apply it to movement solutions in space.

If the task is hard, the mover is under time pressure or high (cognitive) load, System 1 kicks into full gear and it involves a focus on the external effect of the movement. This is called external focus of attention (Schmidt et al. 2019).

System 2 is only available if there are enough resources available. In our context, this may be possible during reflection, explicit coaching, or trying to cognitively solve a movement puzzle, as well as under low-stakes or slow movements. This is the “slow” top-down mode, which comes with the freedom of willingly directing attention. Under System 2, one can place attention on internal processes of the body (as well as on cognition and emotion). During this internal focus of attention, the mover can be focusing on the knee angle, contractions of the working muscle, or their heartbeat. It is about the feedback from the body or the effects on it.

The two types of focus have been studied for decades now, and most scientists agree that when it comes to learning and performance, an external focus should be preferred (Chua et al. 2021). So if one has the goal of learning or teaching a motor skill, attention should be brought to the external effects of the movement. Typically, this may be the default (system 1) path anyway. But under the control of System 2, some movers start worrying a lot about internal processes.

Internal focus of attention has its place. Many embodied practices (mind-body practices) like yoga, traditional martial arts, and some contemporary dancing schools use internal cues in their teachings. The modern mindfulness trend has a strong emphasis on internal processes, and some applied philosophies use internal focus to train right attention or get a better understanding of reality. By placing attention on the internal feedback of the body, one can improve their understanding of bodily sensations. Some theories describe emotions as interpretations of bodily sensations coupled with environmental cues (Craig 2003; Damasio 2004). Embodied practices could, therefore, support behavioral changes by improved processing and awareness of emotions and feelings.

Attention Practice - Let’s get Esoteric

This post it is not just about the scientific, exoteric promises of the practice. It’s about raising curiosity about what is happening to you personally during the proposed practice. In this sense is an esoteric practice, which means that it is research on one’s own internal reality 4. So in this case, the explicit knowledge about what it should be like is not of interest. It is about your participatory knowing of **what is*.*

The proposed practice is an invitation to be playful but serious. Try it out at least twice while staying open-minded and without grapsing for a specific effect.

The practice - A guide

Before starting, make sure to warm up properly and prepare your space and equipment. Turn off your phone or set it aside completely. Prepare your water upfront, if you need that. Everything should be set and ready with minimal external distractions.

Use one or two physical exercises or movement patterns you already know well. You can adapt the practice as you like. At the beginning, I would suggest using equipment like weights or balls because it makes the intended switch of attention much easier. I trained with kettlebells for a while and still use them for this kind of practice. I typically alternate between presses and rows. But that is a personal choice. Look for a challenging weight that you can move about 8 to 20 times with good form. You will do about three to six sets of each exercise without ever going to failure.

Set a timer that signals the start of each new set, so you focus on the actual practice. Try 60 to 180-second intervals, depending on the relative load and the movement. For starters: the heavier the implement, the longer the rest time. There is no need for more details here. Once everything is set up, you can start the practice. Every time the signal goes off, start your set. In between sets, walk a bit or just sit down. So far, it might not be so different from your typical exercise session, but here comes the essential part:

Whenever you do your exercise, your focus should be very narrow and specific. Either envision a target you want to move your implement towards or you want to move it away from. For example, while lifting an object from the ground, you can focus on getting it away from the ground as fast and forcefully as possible. Similarly, you could focus on pressing the implement up towards the ceiling or moving it away from your shirt. Use an external focus and a clear intention that can be expressed in an image (“forceful”, “powerful”, “like a crane”, “like a rocket”, “rip it off the floor”, “throw it far away”…). The moment you finish the exercise, you flip a switch and bring your attention to your bodily sensations. Notice how long it takes for your muscles to “turn off”, how your blood pressure drops, and how breathing regulates. Stay open to whatever rises to consciousness. There are thousands of regulatory processes going on, and some of them are observable if you are curious and open. When the signal goes off again, repeat that cycle. That’s it.

Further recommendations

I recommend a warm up before practice. Nevertheless, this does not mean you have to be optimally prepared. You don’t need a caffeine boost or be fully rested. It is also interesting to play around with your arousal and your level of fatigue. Just be warmed up sufficiently to feel safe with the exercise you choose.

Keep the practice as simple as possible and try it out multiple times. Maybe you can embrace the simplicity. If you feel bored during the session, stay with the boredom. It is fine. Just flip a switch during the next set and focus on the performance during the lift. Then boredom might arise again or not. Both are fine.

Later, you can play around with the exercise selection by using bodyweight exercises, stretching, or even everyday movements like making coffee. You can play around with more challenging environments, too. For now, you might stick to my initial recommendations - equipment to move, medium intensity, and minimal external distractions.

You can turn the whole concept on its head. Switch to an internal focus of attention during the set, and bring your attention to your exteroceptive input (like sight and sounds) during rest. I typically use a broader, more open awareness to contrast with the set’s narrow focus.

For comparison, a similar idea is Joseph Bartz’s Low Sugar Training. He makes it even more raw. But I personally think that some extra instructions help. If you have ever experimented with dance or musical improvisations, you may know by heart how hard it is to move freely without any guidance or boundaries.

Summary of the practice

  • Find a distraction-free environment.
  • Warm-up appropriately.
  • Decide on one or two exercises. Use exercises you have some experience with, and use moderately heavy weights.
  • Set an interval timer to 60-180 seconds for 3-6 sets.
  • Every time the alarm goes off, you do a set of the exercise without going to failure. Leave about 1-3 reps in the tank.
  • During the set, your full attention is focused on an external goal of the movement.
  • You switch to full awareness of internal sensations the moment you put down the implement and stick with it throughout the rest period.
  • Do not add anything to the practice before trying it out at least two to three times.
  • Embrace simplicity and use your curiosity without leaning into any expectations about what the practice will yield.
  • Enjoy!
