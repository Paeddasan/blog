Seminar Fitness and Lifestyle

Last semester, I had the privilege of teaching the course Fitness and Lifestyle at the University of Vienna. Twenty sports and human movement science students attended the course and worked on their teaching and coaching skills in different class settings. Students applied best-practice exercise science to different populations and came up with creative solutions to keep it fun and engaging. During the semester, we collected several insights that I will share in this post. We realized the strength of good organization, worked on guiding the group’s energy, and polished our coaching skills. Our thinking was guided by one overarching question: “Who is in charge?”

Organization

Have a detailed plan but stay flexible

We tend to underestimate the time we need for organization and set-up. Create a fixed schedule for each organizational element, set-up, exercise, and discussions. Plan an extra 20% time for the structural tasks of a class, like preparing the exercises or organizing the group.

There is always something that does not work as planned. When you need special equipment or depend on weather conditions, make sure you have a plan B. At least have a general idea of what to do if that specific equipment is unavailable or broken. This will make it so much easier to stay calm and guide the trainees through an alternative.

What are your non-negotiables? No matter how good your plan is, sometimes you run short on time, or you have another fifteen minutes to fill. Have something up your sleeve for both instances. Check your structure and decide upfront which elements you need and which you could leave out if necessary. Have one more game or exercise to pull out for the end. Make this additional exercise as easy to implement as possible. I would go for a variation of an exercise that you already have in class. Another solution is to combine two prior elements into a series or a complex, which would be a good solution if you focus on motor learning. Depending on the class structure, allowing trainees to experiment with any element they liked during class is also a valuable approach, fostering self-efficacy.

Order is your friend

Sports and training can be chaotic, which is generally a good thing. Chaos keeps us alert and creative. That is why good games always induce some form of chaos. Your job as the trainer is to keep the chaos where it belongs. It cannot spill over into the structure and organization of the class. Keeping everything cleaned up is the most important safety rule in many training scenarios - especially when you are working with older adults or children. Equipment that is not in use immediately comes back where it belongs. You can adopt a very loose, easygoing leadership style if that is your thing. Nevertheless, try to get everyone’s buy-in for keeping the movement space clean and tidy. This can improve safety, responsibility, and group coherence!

Explain effectively

Becoming a good communicator is a key skill in any coaching or training scenario. Have you ever wondered about the announcements on the underground? They always give you the reason for the delay, even if it is just a standard sentence without any actual content: “Due to technical failure, there is currently no service between Schottentor and Wien Mitte.” This does not explain much. But it feels more reasonable to us passengers than just saying there is no train coming. When you explain an exercise, you can use a similar idea.

First, bring up the Why. Give your trainees one brief reason why this particular game or exercise is important.

Second, the announcement is short. Most coaches talk too much! Maybe they lack precise language (at least I sometimes do). Another reason is an overemphasis on details. Find the minimal valuable information your trainees need! For structured exercises, this includes the direct movement goal or intent (“Push the bar towards the ceiling”; “Land with both feet on the yellow line and find your balance for 3 seconds before stepping down.”) and one qualitative criterion, if needed (“Keep your knees locked the whole time”; “Land as silently as a cat.”). For games, the minimal valuable information consists of the task (“Pass the ball without dropping it to the floor”), winning conditions (“Your team wins if you pass it ten times before the other team does so.”), the rule constraints (“No player can catch the ball twice in a row. So when A passes to B, B cannot pass back to A directly.”) and the temporo-spatial constraints (“You have to stay within this area of the room and the game stops whenever one team reaches the win condition.”). For the first round, adding anything else is overkill. Let your trainees practice/play and add further conditions or qualitative criteria 1 for the second set/round.

When working with minimal valuable information, you risk having too little control. Some trainees will explore solutions you did not intend to. It seems like they are breaking the rules - are they? Maybe try reframing this as being creative! Let them explore the movement space and introduce further rules or cues if necessary. Oftentimes the group can help you with that. Remember that you gave them the Why. So they may be able to find the How - which means you acted as a coach and not merely as an instructor.

Energy-flow

Target meaning and activation first

The Why I emphasized earlier is an important element for each class and each individual exercise. As a coach, you often work with additional invisible agendas too. Each element has a meaning that you may explicitly share with the class (“We do this so you get better at catching yourself before a fall.”) and maybe others that you don’t (cooperation, social engagement, fun, …). You may not be able to pick the one meaning that best suits all your trainees all the time. So, if you have the chance, you can also invite them to find their meaning. For example, you can show an exercise and briefly explain the targeted muscular area before asking the group where in their lives or in their sport they might need this movement.

Motivation, meaning, and activation are highly interconnected. Activation includes up- and down-regulation of psycho-physiological arousal. Effective exercise and training must be somewhat demanding. However, relaxation and recovery are as essential for long-term performance and health.

Having meaning in place will partly take care of activation and motivation. But you can still directly influence the group’s energy level explicitly or implicitly. Explicit ways to influence activation include games, drills, and direct manipulations such as breathing or awareness exercises. Implicit ways to influence activation include the way you talk and move and the environment you are in. Use all of them to create an intended flow of energy. Typically, you want to raise the activation during the warm-up, peak for some of the non-negotiable elements of the class, and down-regulate at the end.

Explicit manipulation of activation

A wonderful way to get the group going is fast-paced, interactive games. Tasks with direct performance feedback work well. This category includes coordinative exercises that are difficult enough so people fail if they don’t focus. Competitive elements can be included. However, watch the group’s response (especially from the losing side).

Relaxation techniques have been a major topic in our seminar. But don’t throw them at your trainees unprepared and expect immediate effects. This would be like training the squat once and checking your legs to see if you have grown some muscle. Use relaxation techniques in concert with the general training principles. Incorporate them regularly and teach them systematically. Start in a beginner-friendly environment with little distraction. Later you can change the position (seated instead of lying down), the level of distraction (reduce guidance, change the environment), or the timing (create relaxation in an instant instead of guiding through a 20min full-body relaxation exercise). But all of this has to be learned!

Implicit manipulation of activation

I sometimes find myself showing an exercise to my trainees without the intention and preparation I expect from them. This often leads to similarly loose preparation when they perform the movement. Whenever I do the opposite and, for example, show the whole set-up of a deadlift as if I would go for a demanding working set even with an empty bar, the trainees also tend to approach the exercise with a similar focus and intention. Whatever energy you express during the class may be taken up by the trainees. The way you coach, your posture, voice, and movement - all are important. You can use these as tools to guide the trainees. Speak slowly and calmly during cool-downs, or louder and energetically when you want to push the class. Think about your position in the room and how you walk or demonstrate a movement.

The environment - music, lighting, people watching - can make a difference too. I found dance teachers, but also some yoga teachers, to be the masters of this. The music is not a background element but systematically supports the activity and guides through the class. Some additional caveats should be given here: You cannot serve every music taste. Just make sure not to violate everyone’s eardrums and check your group’s overall response. Think about attention and focus when playing music. If you want to avoid distraction 2, limit vocals and fast-changing structure and style within a song. Both pull attention away from the movement or your coaching. Even if you, yourself, can focus while playing heavy metal, your trainees might not.

Outsource time and timing

The more organizational work you outsource, the more you can focus on coaching, connecting to the trainees, and being fully present and engaged. During interval training, time is an essential part of loading parameters. For those classes, I use the Tabata Timer app on my phone (Android, Apple). If the time is not an integral part of the exercise, set fixed milestones in your class. For example, if you want to have fifteen minutes for a cool down and your class ends at 19:00, write down the actual time (18:45) next to the exercise in your notes or set an alarm on your watch. This way you cannot miss the last element.

The coach alone keeps time! If you master the timing of the class so there is no rushing through the last exercises or losing track of time, you will find that you and the trainees feel secure and relaxed - prerequisites for an engaging exercise session.

Environment - Person - Task

The immediate environment in which the movement takes place gives rise to certain movement options. The surface, the lighting, and the openness of space change movement affordances and constraints, as well as perception during a game. These changes affect motor learning, performance, and injury risk. Potentially, they even drive biological adaptations. In addition, there is a broader perspective to the effects of the environment.

The environment changes how you can coach, communicate, and how the session may feel. When deciding where to locate the training session, consider factors like sun exposure and air quality. Choosing the right environment depends on the group you are working with. While you will not be concerned about the risk of falling on uneven or changing surfaces with children 3, you may choose safety with a group of older adults. At the same time, you may consider clear spatial restrictions when working with children to keep them closer together and allow effective coaching. Social exposure might be another key factor. Some of your trainees might not enjoy training in public. Think about this especially when working with vulnerable groups.

During the seminar, we also discussed the potential effects of Green exercise. Research on mental health and well-being suggests that exposure to natural environments during exercise is superior to indoor or urban exercise interventions. A recent meta-analysis shows benefits on symptoms of anxiety, depression, and distress in clinical and healthy populations (Liang et al. 2026). It is not fully understood how Green exercise improves mental health and well-being, and there was some controversy about the methodological limitations of the research (Laezza et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the research, including studies on green spaces and health (e.g., Kondo et al. 2018), should be considered by coaches. If you can, get outside!

Coaching

Check your own energy

Good trainers or coaches constantly check their group’s well-being and emotional state. They care about how the people in their class are feeling. It also gives constant feedback on how to adjust the exercises, the setting, or when to address certain aspects. Great coaches not only look at their group’s energy, but also check in with themselves. This is harder during a focused and engaging coaching session. But it can be crucial. Handle yourself with as much care as you do your athletes. Your trainees may need some rest or a water break - so do you! You want them to better understand their fatigue and performance by implementing self-ratings - so can you.

When you plan your training session, find one or two slots during which you can check in with yourself. This may not take longer than two to three breaths or briefly closing the eyes. This is probably not the right time for a full reflection on how everything is going. But at least you can center yourself, maybe remember what your intention for the class was or just realize how much joy this work may bring you.

Sometimes you may get tired, confuse the order of the planned exercises, or have to think about scenarios that come up during a game. Don’t worry - every coach has been there! Maybe this is the right moment for a water break or a quick peer-coaching (see below). Take the 2-3 minutes to step back, check your plan, breathe and get back to work.

Concrete, honest feedback

If you have managed most of the points made so far, you are well prepared. You have freed some mental capacity to engage with your group and can focus on effective coaching. Besides motivational support, many trainees want individualized technical feedback. If the exercises and games are well designed, trainees should get immediate feedback on the outcome (i.e., knowledge of outcome). Your job is to give information on their performance (knowledge of performance). Try to be direct and honest when providing feedback. A general “You are doing great!” to the group does not cut it. If you don’t mean it and give no explanation of what exactly they are doing great, it will sound like an empty phrase - which it is. Figure out what is working well and where the person can improve. Also, remember that on the one hand, people may expect a fair amount of direct coaching instructions (“keep the elbow at 90 degrees!”). On the other hand, there is a risk of overcoaching (Janz 2009). So, step back and identify what is really necessary and important before providing your opinion. Even better, create a culture in which trainees can ask for feedback when they think they need it.

Peer-coaching - Everyone is a coach

Compared to one-on-one coaching, groups have several advantages in terms of motor learning and motivation. Instead of seeing groups as having one coach for twenty athletes, we can also view it as having twenty coaches for each trainee. For some parts, this happens automatically. Trainees can watch each other during movements and learn effective and ineffective solutions. Whenever you give direct feedback to one person, others may pick that up too and can learn without making the same mistake themselves. You can also have trainees show a new exercise while you explain it instead of showing it yourself. This all falls under the realm of model-based learning and is highly effective because, in this case, the model is more relatable than the coach. For example, it might give an older person a better perspective of the movement if they see it done by another older person instead of their fit, 25-year-old trainer (more on this here).

You can also try the method of peer-coaching, for example when two trainees pair up to perform an exercise in turns. Now the resting person steps into the role of a coach and gives the working trainee feedback on their form. This not only helps the exercising person. Also, the peer-coach will get a better understanding of the movement by observation, forming explicit knowledge and transforming it into language. In the long run, you should try to improve your athlete’s coaching skills, which will make this method even more effective. Start by giving them checklists for movement quality, add some coaching “rules” like: Always watch from at least two different angles; If the movement is not immediately dangerous, wait till after the movement to give advice; Only correct one thing at a time; Show more - talk less; etc.

With time, peer-coaching will gain momentum, and you can foster an environment of growth and learning. You may also find some hidden strengths when someone who may not be the best athlete turns out to be a valuable coach.

Who is in charge?

Guiding a group of diverse people through an exercise program or creating engaging and fruitful games that are open to everyone is science and art. If you can manage to invoke the joy of moving alone, together with (cooperative) and against (competitive) others while improving people’s fitness, you are doing something powerful and important. Take a step back from time to time to reflect on whom you are doing this for and who is at the center of every action. You may be the coach, and maybe you see yourself as a leader or manager. But ultimately you are part of the group and the class you facilitate. You can all share the experience, the decisions, and the power of moving. Find out which elements of a class should be dictated by you and which elements may be better distributed. You have the bird’s-eye view, but your trainees feel the movement and what it does to them.

Each of the provided points in this article has elements that are centered around the coach and others that are centered around the trainee. These are no perfect solutions but invitations to reflect. Who is it for? Who has the critical information? Who can decide? Who may need support? Coaching is about people’s interactions.
